Intellectual Merit The major environmental and energy-related problems facing our planet directly relate to carbon dioxide and the carbon biogeochemical cycle, which includes the biological fixation of CO2 into organic carbon and the oxidation of fixed carbon back to CO2. The removal and transformation of carbon dioxide in the environment will save our country from future problems that will affect our lives as we know today in our planet. A lot of research has been dedicated to the activation of this molecule reducing it to CO, CO2H2 and HCO2- by electrochemistry, coordination chemistry complexes and/or photochemistry approaches. However, useful anthropogenic catalysts are still not available. In nature, fixation of CO2 occurs on a huge scale, with photosynthetic CO2 fixation occurring at a rate of 200 gigatons per year. Carbon monoxide dehydrogenase (CODH) is a key component of one of six pathways that nature uses to fixate CO2, where this enzyme is capable of reversibly converting CO2 into CO. Even though this system possesses great selectivity, its large size and the anaerobic conditions needed to keep this system active introduce difficulties to the study and development of this enzyme into a solution for our problem.

From previous work, we know that a small ferredoxin (Fd) obtained from Pyrococcus furiosus (Pf) possess a unique aspartate residue, instead of a cysteine in the 14th position, ligating one of the irons in the metal cluster. Upon oxidation, this [Fe4S4]+ Fd loses the Fe ligated by the Asp, making a 3Fe-4S metal cluster. Addition of an excess of Ni(NO3)2 to this protein gives a Ni-bound ferredoxin that is capable of reversible electron transfer and coordination of both a substrate and inhibitor of CODH, CO and cyanide, respectively. Therefore, we believe this NiFd is a protein-based model for CODH (Figure 1). Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) together with Mossbauer studies revealed insights into the electronic structure of this system. Two electronic configurations, S=1/2 and S=3/2, were observed across independent samples when [NiFe3S4]+-CO was studied, possibly due to spin or redox isomers from an equilibrium process. [NiFe3S4]+-CN, on the other hand, shows a spin state of 3/2. A computational study might help with predicting structural-electronic configurations required for the reactivity of this NiFd model to further our understanding of CO2 reduction activity in CODH.

Computational studies provided more evidence to support the coordination of CN- and CO to the nickel center in wild-type (WT) [NiFe3S4]+ Fd. We also observe coordination of CO2 in the doublyreduced state. In this structure, CO2 appears bent, suggesting reductive activation (Figure 2). An interesting observation from this model is that when coordinating substrates or inhibitor, the metal cluster opens, assuming a geometry resembling that of the open cubane in the CODH active site. Moreover, in the case of CO2 binding, one of the oxygen atoms coordinates one of the iron centers in the cluster, similar to the CODH active site when it coordinates CO2 in the same redox state. This cluster opening results in the loss of a nickel-sulfur bond from inside the metal cluster. Combining these results with previously reported work supports the hypothesis that the nickel-substituted Fd serves as a protein-based model of CODH.

Insight into the enzymatic mechanism will be obtained through understanding the roles of the first and second coordination sphere in CO2 reduction. Mutations to these residues will provide information on what structural properties are needed for our system to activate CO2 and stabilize the intermediate states of the catalysts. The D14C mutation increases the similarities between Fd and the first coordination sphere of the CODH active site, in which all the protein ligands to the cubane are Cys residues. Geometry optimization of this system across different redox states were studied, showing D14C NiFd to behave similarly to WT NiFd. D14C NiFd retains the ability to coordinate both the CO substrate and CN- inhibitor of CODH in a similar manner. One of the [NiFe3S4]0-CO2 models shows Cys14 losing coordination to the Ni atom. We believe this could be part of the mechanism, but additional studies are required to test this computational observation. A D14A mutation will be studied to assess if an open coordination site will be more beneficial to coordinate a CO2 molecule. From previous X-ray crystallography studies, we know that a His residue in the second coordination sphere stabilizes the activated carbon monoxide in the intermediate state through H-bonding and electrostatic interactions. Therefore, a D14H mutant will be investigated as well. Another structural difference between NiFd and CODH’s active site is the presence of a dangler iron. Previous crystallography and spectroscopically studies demonstrate the importance of this iron in the CO2 activation reaction. An experimental attempt to reproduce this iron center will be one of the goals of the research project, with modifications to the computational model serving as the first step of the process.

Spectroscopy is assessed in these cases to validate the computational model. The parameters measured in these techniques, like Mossbauer and EPR, can be calculated using DFT on our computational model (Table 1). By comparing the experimental and calculated data, we can justify our computational model as a good representation of the real system. However, this is just the beginning of a journey into understanding the electronic state of this system. Together with spectroscopic measurements, we will further our understanding of this cluster to predict reactivity and the conditions to achieved it. This opens the doors to reveal the reasons for the high efficiency of the enzymatic reactivity demonstrated by CODH towards CO2 reduction. X-ray absorption and Infrared spectroscopy are other techniques that will be used to justify the computational model, with comparison to experimental data and to study molecular orbitals responsible for this electronic and vibrational transitions in the XAS and IR, respectively. Previous work studied the vibrational frequencies of [NiFe3S4]+-CN and correlated these results to a square planar binding geometry. QM/MM previous research suggest a tetrahedral distorted binding geometry of the nickel in CODH-CN. With our computational model, broadening this to include CO and CO2, is in order. Cyclic voltammetry will be assessed to study reversibility of the electron transfer after the different mutations mentioned above. Laviron plots to investigate electron transfer kinetics and pH studies to understand proton transfer for these systems will be performed to investigate if these systems follow PCET just like CODH. Ultimately, development towards an active CO2-reducing catalyst will be pursued by studying this new NiFd protein-based model.
